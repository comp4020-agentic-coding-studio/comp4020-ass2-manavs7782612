Rye has gluten proteins and cannot make gluten. That single fact undoes most of
what you have been doing since week 1: nothing to knead, no windowpane to look
for, no dough to shape. What holds a rye loaf up is a gel. What stops the
flour's own enzymes from eating that gel in the oven is acid, and that is why
this week's sour is structural rather than a matter of taste.

## Before the session

Build the sour 12 to 16 hours before you come in, and build it warm: 180 g
wholemeal rye flour, 180 g water at 35 °C, 18 g of your wheat starter. Your
starter is the seed here, not the sour. It carries the culture across; the rye
and the warmth decide what that culture does.

Warmth is the part people skip. You have been keeping your starter near 24 °C
since week 6 because that balances acetic and lactic acid nicely in a wheat
levain. A rye sour wants 26--28 °C, because you are not after balance --- you
are after a pH near 4.3 before the dough goes anywhere near an oven. An oven
with the light on, an esky with a jar of hot water in it, or the top of the
fridge if your kitchen runs warm.

It will not dome. It looks like porridge with holes in it, and it is ready when
the surface has risen and just begun to sink in the middle. Week 6 drilled you
to catch a levain before it sank, because a sunken levain has spent its gas.
Here the gas was never the job. Let it sink and take the acid. Taste it: sharp
enough to make you pull a face is right, and mildly tangy means two more hours
or two more degrees.

Decide your rye percentage before you arrive. The bench formula is 60% rye and
40% bread flour, which is the middle of the range and the one to take if this
is your first. A 30% rye behaves almost like the loaves you already know and is
worth doing if you want a comparison; a 100% wholegrain Vollkornbrot uses this
same method with more water, a longer ferment and a bake measured in hours. Buy
wholemeal rye flour, not a bagged "rye blend", which is mostly wheat and will
teach you nothing.

Read the [week 7 lecture](/lectures/week-07/) first. The bench work is four
minutes of mixing and a lot of waiting, and the waiting only makes sense if you
know what the acid is doing.

## In the session

Three hours, and almost none of it is handling.

| Component     | Weight | Bakers' % |
| ------------- | ------ | --------- |
| Wholemeal rye | 360 g  | 60%       |
| Bread flour   | 240 g  | 40%       |
| Water         | 480 g  | 80%       |
| Salt          | 12 g   | 2%        |

Half the rye and 180 g of the water are already in your sour, so the final mix
is the sour plus 180 g rye, 240 g bread flour, 300 g water at 40 °C and 12 g
salt.

**Mix, four minutes.** Wet a spatula or wet one hand and work everything
together until no dry flour is left and the paste is uniform. Then stop. There
is no network to develop, so time past homogeneous buys nothing; it warms the
dough and it tempts you to correct the stickiness with flour, which is how a
rye ends up short of the water its pentosans need. Aim for 28--30 °C out of the
bowl. At 80% hydration this will look and behave like thick wet plaster, and
that is correct. Every year someone tips it onto the bench and kneads it. It
tears into sticky rags, picks up half the bench flour, and there is no way
back --- there was never a network to break, only a gel to wreck.

**Bulk, 45 minutes at 28 °C.** Covered, undisturbed, no folds. It will not
double and you should not wait for it to. You are looking for a surface that
has domed slightly and lost its wet sheen.

**Into the tin.** Grease a 900 g tin --- the same tin as week 1, taking about
1090 g this time against that loaf's 840 g piece, because a rye rises perhaps a
third and has to start nearer the top. Scrape the whole mass in with a wet
spatula, push it into the corners, and smooth the top with a wet hand until it
is as flat as icing. You are filling a tin, not shaping a loaf --- surface
tension does nothing for you here. Dust the top with rye flour.

**Proof, 45 to 75 minutes at 28 °C.** The dusted surface is your gauge. Fine
cracks opening across it mean the gas underneath has expanded as far as the gel
will let it, and that is the moment to bake. Wide gaping fissures and a domed
centre mean you are late; the loaf will sink into a dense band along the base
in the oven.

**Bake, 250 °C with steam for 15 minutes, then vent and drop to 190 °C for
another 45 to 60.** The long tail at lower heat is the point. A rye crumb is
dense and wet, it sets from the outside in, and the starch needs sustained heat
to finish gelatinising all the way to the middle. Pull it at 96--98 °C in the
centre. A 2 kg Vollkornbrot wants three hours, most of it under 160 °C, and a
pumpernickel sits at 120 °C overnight --- which is where that near-black crumb
comes from, not from anything added to it.

## Afterwards

Do not cut it. Twenty-four hours is the minimum and 48 is better, and this is
the rule this class breaks every year. The crumb is still setting as it cools:
the starch gel is retrograding and binding water that is currently free, and
until that finishes the loaf is a wet paste with a crust on it. Cut one at two
hours and it smears down the blade in a grey band, closes up behind the knife,
and there is nothing left to assess. Cool it out of the tin on a rack, then wrap
it in a tea towel once it is properly cold --- plastic on a warm rye softens the
crust for nothing.

Slice it thin, 5 to 8 mm, with a long serrated knife. Rye is eaten in thin
slices because that is the thickness the crumb reads well at, not out of
restraint.

Log the sour the way week 6 had you write feeds --- three numbers, 1:10:10,
double the largest ratio that week gave you --- along with its temperature and
hours. Then dough temperature out of the bowl, the time from tin to first
cracks, the total bake, and the hour you finally cut it.
Photograph the crumb. The gummy, unset centre that
[bake-off 2](/assessments/bake-off-2-fermentation/) marks you down for looks
identical whether it came from a weak sour or an impatient knife, and your log
is the only thing that tells the two apart.
