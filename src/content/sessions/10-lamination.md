Last week the butter went into the dough and disappeared into the crumb. This
week it stays out of the dough, in one cold slab, and the whole bake is an
argument about temperature that you can lose at four separate points.

## Before the session

Mix the détrempe the night before. 500 g bread flour, 200 g water, 100 g milk,
50 g sugar, 10 g salt, 7 g instant yeast and 25 g soft butter — 60% liquid, 10%
sugar, 2% salt, 5% butter on flour weight. Mix it to a shaggy, barely developed
dough and then stop. You will roll this thing six or seven times tomorrow, and
every pass develops gluten; a dough that arrives fully kneaded fights you on the
first fold and tears on the third.

Press it into a 20 × 20 cm square about 2 cm thick, wrap it, and refrigerate it
overnight. Flat chills evenly. A ball chills from the outside in and hands you a
warm middle you cannot see until it smears.

Buy the butter deliberately: unsalted, European-style, 82 to 84% fat, 250 g,
which is half your flour weight. Supermarket block butter is 80%, and most of
that missing two to four per cent is water dispersed through the fat. More water
means a harder, more brittle block at fridge temperature — it snaps when you
bend it where the high-fat block yields. You cannot roll a sheet out of
something that snaps.

Bring a ruler and a bench scraper. The measurements in this session are not
decoration. A butter block 2 cm out of square is a butter block sitting under
the edge of the dough, and it comes out the side on the second fold.

## In the session

The room is held at 18 to 19 °C and there is a fridge shelf for every bench.
Both of those are equipment.

**Two components, then one.** The détrempe is the yeasted dough you brought. The
beurrage is the butter block: pound the 250 g between two sheets of baking paper
into a 16 × 16 cm square roughly 1 cm thick, working it with the pin until it
bends without cracking, then chill it back to 16 °C. Roll the détrempe out to
20 × 36 cm, seat the butter square on one half with a 2 cm margin on the three
open sides, fold the bare half over it and pinch that margin shut. That is the
lock-in. From here the butter is enclosed, and everything you do to the dough
you also do to the butter.

**The window.** Butter is only pliable between about 16 and 20 °C. Below that,
too much of the fat is crystalline and the block behaves like a biscuit: it
shatters under the pin into shards that puncture the dough, and you see grey
flecks breaking the surface. Above it, the crystal network gives up, the butter
smears and is absorbed into the flour around it, and your two components become
one. Both failures end lamination. Check the block with a probe at every
lock-in, and when the dough starts to feel soft under your hands, stop rolling
and put it back in the fridge.

**The folds.** Roll the packet to about 20 × 60 cm and 8 mm thick, working away
from you and back along the same axis every time. Rolling sideways stretches
some layers and not others, and uneven layers bake into a lopsided crumb. Then a
letter fold — thirds, like a business letter — and into the fridge for 30 to 60
minutes. That rest does two jobs at once: the gluten relaxes so the next roll
does not fight back, and the butter comes back to the top of its window.

Three letter folds multiply out to 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 layers of butter. A fourth
gives 81, and 81 is worse. Sheeted to 3.5 mm, the whole repeat — one butter
layer and the dough under it — is down around four hundredths of a millimetre,
thinner than a rolled dough can hold. The layers tear, merge into their
neighbours, and you have done an hour of extra work to bake something bready. We
stop at three.

**Cut and roll.** Halve the packet and sheet each half to 3 to 4 mm, around
25 × 60 cm, rested 20 minutes cold so it does not spring back while you
measure.
Triangles 12 cm at the base and 25 cm tall, alternating point up and point down
so nothing is wasted: ten to a strip, twenty out of the packet, about 55 g each.
Notch the base, stretch the triangle gently to length, roll from base to tip and
finish with the tip tucked underneath.

**The proof that catches everyone.** 24 to 26 °C, two to two and a half hours.
Warm enough to move the yeast and strictly below the temperature at which your
butter stops being a solid. A proofer at 30 °C does look faster, and it is: the
butter runs out of the layers, pools on the paper, and you bake a dense roll in
a puddle of its own structure. Shake the tray at the end — a ready croissant
wobbles as one soft mass, and you can see daylight between the layers at the cut
face.

**The bake.** 200 to 220 °C for 18 to 20 minutes. Butter is 16 to 18% water, and
at that oven temperature the water in each sheet flashes to steam and drives the
dough above it away from the dough below it. The yeast set the volume overnight;
the steam sets the structure in the first four minutes.

Cut one open at the bench while it is still warm. The honeycomb — irregular open
cells, a spiral you can follow from the outside in — is the record of whether
the layers survived the last five hours.

| What you cut into | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Bready crumb, no spiral you can trace | Butter went below 16 °C and shattered through the dough |
| Dense, heavy, one solid mass | Butter went above 20 °C and merged into the détrempe |
| Collapsed shell, butter pooled on the tray | Proofed too warm, or too long |

## Afterwards

Weigh the tray paper before and after the bake. Leaked butter is a number, not
an impression, and 12 g on the paper tells you more about your proofer than any
amount of looking at the croissants does.

Log the temperature of the butter at lock-in and at each of the three folds, the
room temperature, the length of each rest, your final sheet thickness, and the
proof temperature and time. Change one of those next time, not four.
