A bagel dough is 55% hydration on flour with 14% protein, and it will fight the
mixer for twelve minutes before it agrees to be a dough at all. Week 3 went
looking for the wettest dough you could still handle. This is the other end of
the same dial.

## Before the session

The dough is made the night before, not here. Mix it at home, shape it, and
give it 12 to 24 hours at 4 °C. Nothing in the session works if you turn up
with a bowl of fresh dough.

Buy high-gluten flour at 13–14% protein. Bakers' flour at 11.5–13% will make
something bagel-shaped, but the chew will be soft and you will know. Buy barley
malt syrup too — the non-diastatic kind, which is cooked so its enzymes are
dead. It is about two-thirds maltose, and it does three jobs at once: colour, a
dark malty flavour that nothing else supplies, and a little sugar the yeast can
eat in a dough that has no other sweetener.

Per 500 g of flour: 275 g water, 10 g salt, 3 g instant yeast, 25 g malt syrup.
That is 813 g of dough and eight bagels. Mix it stiff. In a stand mixer that is
ten to twelve minutes on low, and the machine will walk across the bench if you
let it; by hand it is twenty minutes with two rests, because dry gluten
hydrates slowly and there is not enough water in there to lubricate the strands
while they link up. You are still looking for a windowpane; it just takes twice
as long to get one. Finish at 24–25 °C. Bulk for forty-five minutes only — the
real fermentation happens in the fridge.

Divide into eight pieces of about 100 g. Shape them by either route: roll a
rope 26 cm long, wrap it round the back of your hand so the ends overlap by
4 cm, and roll that seam against the bench until you cannot see it; or round the
piece tight, drive a thumb through the centre, and stretch the ring on two
fingers. Whichever you use, open the hole to about 5 cm. That looks absurd. It
will not look absurd tomorrow, because the ring swells in the boil and again in
the oven, and a hole that looked right on the bench closes into a navel.

Rings go on a semolina-dusted tray and into the fridge uncovered. The cold buys
flavour, as it did in week 4, and it does a second job here: it firms the ring
and dries the surface to a skin, so the shape survives ninety seconds in moving
water. Cover them and you get the flavour without the skin.

Read the [week 8 lecture](/lectures/week-08/) before you come in, so the boil
makes sense while you are standing over the pot.

## In the session

Three hours, and the pot is the only queue. Float-test first: drop one ring
into a bowl of cold water. If it surfaces within ten seconds it has enough gas
in it to be worth boiling. If it sinks and stays down, it is under-proofed:
leave the tray at room temperature for twenty to thirty minutes and test
another. Boiling an under-proofed ring gives you a dense puck with a pale,
tight crumb, and the oven cannot undo that.

Get a wide pot of water to a steady roll — 4 L, with 60 g of malt syrup stirred
through it. Four rings at a time, 45 seconds a side. They will sink for a
moment, then rise.

The water gelatinises the starch at the surface into a continuous skin, and
that skin is the whole bread. It seals the ring, so oven spring is cut short
and the crumb sets dense and chewy instead of opening out. It comes out of the
pot glossy and tacky, which is the only reason seeds stay on. And its pH
decides the crust colour: malt water is near neutral and browns to a warm tan;
a tablespoon of bicarbonate per litre lifts it to about pH 8.5 and gives a
darker, faintly soapy pretzel note; food-grade lye goes past pH 13 for a deep
mahogany crust, and we do not use it on this bench.

Do not leave them in. Past about two minutes a side the skin over-gelatinises
and dehydrates, and the bagel bakes out leathery with a wrinkled, elephant-hide
surface — as does one that went into the pot over-proofed. Lift them straight
onto a rack for thirty seconds, then seed them wet, both sides, pressing into a
plate of sesame or poppy.

Bake at 225 °C on a tray for eighteen to twenty-two minutes. No steam. Week 2
used steam to hold the crust soft so the loaf could keep expanding; the boiled
skin has already settled how much expansion you get, and steam now would only
keep the surface from drying to a gloss. Turn the tray at ten minutes. The
centre should read 96 °C and the base should be a shade darker than the top.

## Afterwards

Cool them twenty minutes before you cut. The chew is still setting, and a bagel
cut hot tastes gummy in a way it will not taste an hour later.

Log the same four numbers as always, plus two more this week: hours in the
fridge, and seconds in the pot. Those are the two variables that move a bagel
most, and you will want the record.
