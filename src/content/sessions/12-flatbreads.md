Eleven weeks of getting dough into an oven, and this one does without. A
flatbread is cooked on a surface: heat conducted into one face at a time, at
temperatures a domestic oven cannot reach, for times measured in seconds. The
tin, the stone, the steam in a closed box, the hour of proving — today you find
out how much of that apparatus was ever load-bearing.

## Before the session

Everyone makes pita and roti. Then pick one of naan or corn tortilla; between
the two of you on a bench, do both and swap.

Mix the pita dough at home the day before. Per person: 300 g bakers' flour,
180 g water, 6 g salt, 3 g instant yeast, 15 g olive oil — 60% hydration, 2%
salt, 1% yeast, 5% oil. Ten minutes of mixing to a smooth dough, finish at
24 °C, bulk ninety minutes, then divide into six pieces of about 80 g and round
them. Bring them covered on a floured tray. They will sit four hours at room
temperature or overnight at 4 °C.

Buy atta for the roti. It is stone-milled wholemeal wheat, ground much finer
than the wholemeal in a supermarket baking aisle and a great deal thirstier —
it takes 65 to 70% water where a coarse wholemeal at the same figure stays
gritty and tears when you roll it. If you are doing tortillas, buy masa harina,
and check the bag says *nixtamalizado*. Cornflour and polenta are not
substitutes; one is pure starch and the other is the wrong grind, and neither
will hold together. For naan, full-fat plain yoghurt and ghee or a neutral oil.

Kit: a rolling pin, tongs, and a dry heavy pan — cast iron, carbon steel or a
baking steel. Thin non-stick will lose fifty degrees the moment a cold disc
lands on it, and there are no fifty degrees to spare this week.

Read the [week 12 lecture](/lectures/week-12/) first. The bench work is fast
and mostly irreversible, and there is no time to work out what is happening
while it happens.

## In the session

Three hours. Heat the surface first; everything else waits on it.

A baking steel in a 250 °C oven needs forty minutes. A cast-iron pan on the
largest burner needs ten. Test it by flicking water across the face: the drops
should skitter and disappear, not sit and simmer. Simmering means you are below
about 200 °C and the pita will fail.

**Pita.** Roll one ball at a time. Work from the centre outwards, quarter-turn
between passes, to 20 cm across and 3 mm thick, and run a finger over four
points around the disc before you stop — an even thickness is the entire
exercise. Onto the steel it goes bare, no oil, no tray.

Here is what happens next. Both faces of the disc reach 100 °C within about
twenty seconds and set into thin skins while the middle is still cool, wet
dough. Then the water in that middle boils. Steam occupies roughly 1600 times
the volume of the water it came from, it is produced faster than it can escape
through two set skins, and it forces them apart. Two to four minutes and the
pita is a balloon that collapses into a pocket as it cools. In a pan rather
than an oven, set the first face for thirty seconds, flip, and the lift comes
on the second side.

Three ways it goes wrong, and you will see all three on the bench today. Rolled
unevenly, the thin half sets first and the steam vents sideways through the
half still soft: half a pocket and a dense lip. Rolled too thick, the centre
never reaches 100 °C before the outside is cooked. On a cool surface the disc
dries from the outside in and browns without ever inflating, and you have made
a cracker. None of those three is a fault in the recipe.

**Roti.** 200 g atta, 130 g water, 4 g salt. No yeast, no starter, no baking
powder, nothing. Knead five minutes and rest thirty, and the rest is not
negotiable: the bran needs the time to take up water, and the gluten needs it
to relax enough that a 2 mm disc stays 2 mm instead of springing back to 3.
Divide into six, roll each to 16 cm.

Tawa dry and hot, around 230 °C. Thirty seconds on the first side until pale
spots show, flip, forty seconds on the second while you press the edges with a
folded cloth, then lift it with tongs straight onto the open flame. It balloons
in three or four seconds. That is the pita mechanism again with nothing
fermenting anywhere in the dough — steam is the only leavening this bread has
ever had. Roll one at 4 mm as a control. It will brown, it will cook through,
and it will lie flat, because there is too much dough between the faces for the
middle to reach boiling before the skins are done.

**Naan.** On the bench: 300 g plain flour, 120 g full-fat yoghurt, 60 g water,
15 g ghee, 6 g salt, 5 g sugar, 3 g instant yeast. That is week 9's argument in
a flat shape — fat coats the gluten strands and shortens them, so the bread
tears tender instead of chewing. The yoghurt brings acid as well as fat: near
pH 4.5 it slackens the gluten, and its lactose and milk protein brown faster
and darker than a lean dough would. Bulk ninety minutes, divide into five, roll
into teardrops about 5 mm thick.

A tandoor runs at 400 to 480 °C. The naan is slapped onto the clay wall, sticks
because its underside is wet, and comes off in sixty to ninety seconds
blistered and charred in patches. We have no tandoor, so: pan as hot as it
goes, brush one face of the naan with water, lay it wet-side down so it grips,
lid on for a minute until the top is domed with bubbles, then turn the whole
pan upside down over the burner and let the flame catch those bubbles for
twenty seconds.

**Tortilla.** Corn first: 200 g masa harina, 260 g warm water, 3 g salt, mixed
and rested ten minutes. Flatten a ball under your palm — if the edge cracks the
dough is dry, and 10 g of water at a time will fix it. Press to 2 mm between
two sheets of plastic. Comal at about 200 °C: fifty seconds, flip, forty
seconds, flip again, and on that third turn it should puff.

Nothing in that dough is gluten and nothing ever was. What holds it together is
nixtamalisation. The maize is simmered and then steeped in a calcium hydroxide
solution at around pH 12, which dissolves the pericarp, partly gelatinises the
starch and lets the wet grain grind into a cohesive masa. The same alkaline
cook frees the grain's niacin, which in untreated maize is bound up as niacytin
and passes straight through you, and it lifts the calcium and the available
amino acids as well. Maize crossed the Atlantic in the sixteenth century
without the alkaline cook attached to it, and pellagra followed.

The flour tortilla makes the opposite case: 300 g plain flour, 50 g lard or
shortening, 180 g warm water, 6 g salt. Rub the fat in, mix, rest thirty
minutes, roll to 2 mm, forty seconds a side. This one has gluten, and the fat
is there to keep it short. That is why a flour tortilla folds cold without
cracking and a corn one, held up by gelatinised starch alone, snaps.

## Afterwards

Stack them in a clean tea towel as they come off the heat. The trapped steam
keeps the skins pliable; a pita cooled naked on a rack goes rigid within the
hour and will not open.

Worth a look back, because today undoes most of it. Week 1 built a gluten
network and weeks 2 to 5 mainly argued about how much water to put in it. Week
6 swapped one yeast strain for a population. Week 7 took the gluten away
entirely and left you holding a pentosan gel that only acid could protect. Week
8 boiled a gelatinised skin onto the outside, week 9 coated the strands in fat
and egg, week 10 laminated butter between them, week 11 replaced the biology
with a chemical reaction that starts the moment it gets wet. This morning's
roti has none of that — no culture, no yeast, no fat, no tin, no oven — and it
still inflates. Structure comes from whatever is available. The only question
worth asking of a dough is which thing is holding it up: gluten, pentosans,
gelatinised starch, steam or a leavening salt. Answer that and the decisions
about temperature, hydration and time mostly answer themselves.

Log the surface temperature, the seconds per side and whether each bread
inflated. Then read [bake-off 3: the showpiece](/assessments/bake-off-3-showpiece/),
which is due on Friday 28 May, a fortnight after teaching ends. Twelve weeks of
logs are the material you build it from.
