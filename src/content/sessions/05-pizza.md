A Neapolitan base ferments for two days and bakes in ninety seconds. The dough
is deliberately plain — flour, water, salt and almost no yeast — because at
480 °C the oven supplies everything else.

## Before the session

Mix on Saturday morning for Monday. Per person: 500 g type 00 flour, 310 g
water, 13 g salt and 0.5 g instant yeast. That is 62% hydration, 2.6% salt and
0.1% yeast, and the yeast figure is not a typo — weigh it on a jeweller's scale
or scale the batch up and take a share.

Dissolve the salt in the water, add the flour, and mix for three or four
minutes, only until there are no dry patches. Do not knead it. Leave it covered
at room temperature for two hours, divide into four 250 g balls, seal each one
tight against the bench, and put them in the fridge at 4 °C for 24 to 48 hours.
Take them out four hours before the bench so they come up to 20 °C and finish
proving.

You cold-retarded focaccia last week for flavour. Here the fridge is also doing
the lifting: at 0.1% yeast the dough needs those two days simply to rise, and
the slow rise is what makes it extensible enough to open with your fingers.

Type 00 is ground from soft wheat, and ground gently, so it carries less
damaged starch than bread flour. Damaged starch is what amylase can get at, so
this dough makes sugar slowly and finishes two days of fermentation with very
little of it left over. That matters on Monday. A sugary dough in a 480 °C oven
goes dark all over in the time the rim takes to rise; this one browns where the
heat is worst and stays pale everywhere else.

After ciabatta and focaccia, 62% will feel tight and almost dry in your hands.
That is the point. A wet dough will not come off a peel in one piece, and a
slack base drops its rim on the way into the oven.

Bring your four balls in a lidded box and nothing else. Tomato and fior di latte
are on the bench.

## In the session

Four hours, four bakes each, and most of the learning happens in the ninety
seconds you cannot intervene in.

Open the ball on a bed of semolina. Press your fingertips into the centre and
walk them outward, stopping 2 cm short of the edge — you are driving gas out of
the middle and into the rim, which is where you want it. Then lift the disc onto
the backs of your knuckles and let it widen under its own weight, turning as you
go, until it is 28 to 30 cm across, 2 to 3 mm in the centre and a plump 1.5 cm
at the edge.

There is no rolling pin on this bench. A pin flattens the rim you just spent two
days inflating, and because it thins the centre unevenly it leaves you a
membrane that tears — usually at the worst moment, halfway off the peel.

Dress it with 70 g of crushed tomato spread to within 2 cm of the rim and 80 g
of fior di latte, torn and drained on paper for twenty minutes first. Drained is
the whole instruction. Cheese straight from the brine carries water that has
nowhere to go in ninety seconds except down, and a soggy middle is the most
common failure on this bench by a wide margin.

The bench oven runs at 480 °C. Here is what that buys, against the ovens you
have at home:

| Oven | Floor | Bake | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wood or gas, 480 °C | ~430 °C | 60–90 s | Puffed rim, leopard spots, a base soft enough to fold |
| Domestic, 250 °C, on steel | ~250 °C | 5–7 min | Good colour, crisp through, rim dry rather than custardy |
| Domestic, 250 °C, bare tray | ~180 °C | 12–15 min | Pale underside, biscuit texture, rim that never lifted |

Colour at this speed is two reactions, not one. Maillard browning — amino acids
and reducing sugars rearranging from around 140 °C — turns the pale ground of
the rim gold and tastes of bread. The dark spots are past that: a bubble of gas
pushes the skin up into the hottest air in the oven, its surface passes 300 °C,
and the sugars and starch pyrolyse to carbon. Leoparding is a controlled burn —
coin-sized, faintly bitter, scattered across a pale field. A continuous black
underside is the same reaction with nobody watching it.

A wood oven normally runs its dome hotter than its floor, and you want it
that way. If your base browns before the rim has risen, the floor has got ahead
of the air. Shortening the bake will not fix it; you will get a raw rim on a burnt
base. Let the floor shed heat between pizzas, or lift the pizza back onto the
peel and hold it up near the dome for the last twenty seconds.

At home you will not reproduce this, and you should stop trying. Six minutes is
long enough for the interior to lose the moisture that makes a cornicione
custardy, so a 250 °C oven gives you a crisp pizza rather than a Neapolitan one.
A steel closes more of the gap than a stone, because steel conducts heat an
order of magnitude faster and dumps it into the base in the first thirty seconds
instead of trickling it in. Preheat either one for a full hour at maximum, then
switch to the grill element for the last two minutes so the top catches up
before the base overbakes.

## Afterwards

Start a sourdough starter today. Week 6 is sourdough, and a starter begun next
Monday will not be ready for it.

In a clean jar, mix 50 g wholemeal flour with 50 g water at about 28 °C. Cover
it loosely and keep it somewhere near 24 °C. From tomorrow, once a day, throw
away all but 25 g and feed it 25 g flour and 25 g water at the same temperature.
Days two and three will smell sharp and rise hardly at all — that is the wrong
bacteria winning early, and it passes. By week 6 it should double within four to
six hours of a feed and smell of yogurt and green apple. A nail-polish smell
means it is starving, so feed it twice a day for the last stretch.

Fermentation is the thread from here to week 8: today's two-day cold rise, then
sourdough, rye and bagels, all of it marked by
[bake-off 2](/assessments/bake-off-2-fermentation/). Keep the starter log from
today — feed times, how far it rose, how long it took. That log is evidence, and
you cannot reconstruct it in week 8.

Before you leave, photograph the underside of your best base and your worst one
side by side, and write one line on which of the three went wrong: the tear, the
flood, or the floor that ran ahead of the air.
