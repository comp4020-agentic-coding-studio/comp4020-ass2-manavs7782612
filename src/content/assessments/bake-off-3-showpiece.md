## The brief

> Design a bread you would be happy to see in a shop window, bake it for the
> bake-off on 28 May, and justify every decision in it against the twelve weeks
> behind you.

You set the brief and then you answer it. Weeks 9 to 12 are the freshest
material and the obvious place to look — brioche, challah, croissant, banana
bread, flatbreads — but nothing rules out a rye, a sourdough, or the tin loaf
from week 1 if you can say why that was the difficult choice. Choose on the
decision you want to be questioned about, not on the bread you already bake
well.

Three things have to be explicit in the proposal and again at the bench. First,
the structural system: gluten network, pentosan gel, gelatinised starch,
laminated butter, chemical leavening. Say which one carries the load in your
bread and what the others are doing around it. Second, the fermentation
schedule — the ratio, the temperatures, the hours, and why it is that shape and
not a faster one. Third, the oven: steam or dry, held heat or falling, stone,
tin or lid, and what each of those is for. A student who wants the crumb of a
36-hour retarded dough and the schedule of a straight-dough Tuesday has not
finished designing anything.

Scope is where a showpiece usually goes wrong. You propose on 3 May and bake on
28 May: twenty-five days, and the last eighteen of them have no class in them.
That is one trial bake and one real one if nothing goes wrong. A laminated
brioche with a cooked filling and a plaited crown is three doughs, two chills
and a shaping problem you have never met, and it will eat double the hours the
recipe suggests. Cost it out before you lodge the proposal: write the hours
beside each stage, add the oven time you can actually get to, and see whether
the total fits in twenty-five days. Idris will tell you at Bake 11 if it does
not. Hearing it then is cheap. Discovering it at 2am on the Thursday is not.

Flatbreads land at Bake 12, the week after you propose. If that is where you
are heading, propose it on the logs you already have and expect the detail to
move once you have baked one on a dry surface.

One bread, and only one. Not a bread with two things beside it: a board of
accompaniments is a different assessment, and every hour it takes is an hour
off the bread.

## What you submit

Three things: one at Bake 11, two on the day.

**The proposal**, one page, on paper, handed to Idris at Bake 11 on 3 May. What
you are making, the structural system and why, the fermentation schedule with
its temperatures and hours, the oven plan, and an hour estimate broken down by
stage. Idris signs it, or sends it back for a smaller version — a revised page
reaches him by that Friday, 7 May. A bake with no signed proposal is judged
against nothing and cannot pass.

**The showpiece**, whole and uncut, on the bench by 12:00 on Friday 28 May,
baked within twenty-four hours of it. Bake it wherever you can get an oven and
bring it finished; there is no oven time on the day. If it is a flatbread and
comes in numbers, bring six. Marisol cuts it in front of you and you answer two
or three questions about whatever the slice shows — the crumb, the lamination,
the sourness. A bread that arrives as photographs is not marked.

**The bake sheet**, one page, as a PDF uploaded before you walk in. The final
formula in bakers' percentages including any prefermented flour, the schedule
as it actually ran with clock times and dough temperatures, the oven
temperature and what you did about steam, and a short paragraph on where the
bake departed from the proposal and what you did about it. Departures are
expected. Unrecorded ones read as guesswork.

[The Bake pages](/sessions/) hold every formula and schedule you were given,
and twelve weeks of your own logs hold the rest. If illness or an oven failure
is going to stop you, read the [assessment policies](/policies/) before Friday
rather than after it.
