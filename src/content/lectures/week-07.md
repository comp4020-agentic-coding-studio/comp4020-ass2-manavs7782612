Rye flour contains prolamins and glutelins, the same two protein classes that
make gluten in wheat. Wet it and mix it and you get no gluten. The reason is a
group of carbohydrates called pentosans, or arabinoxylans: 6 to 8% of rye
flour by weight against 2 to 3% in wheat, and able to hold something like ten
times their own weight in water. They hydrate first, they hydrate fast, and
they leave the proteins short of water and physically in the way of each other.
The protein chains never get close enough, or mobile enough, to polymerise into
a continuous network.

So the structure of a rye loaf is made of something else: a viscous gel of
hydrated pentosans, plus starch that gelatinises during the bake and sets around
it. Both of those are consequences of heat and water, not of work. This is why
nothing you learned about developing a dough transfers. There is no windowpane
because there is no film to pull. Kneading does not build strength, it only
tears the gel and smears the starch. An 80% hydration rye is a paste, and it is
supposed to be.

That leaves a problem the gluten network was quietly solving for you. Rye
carries far more active alpha-amylase than wheat does, and rye starch begins
gelatinising near 55 °C, a few degrees below the point at which wheat's sets
the crumb of a week 1 loaf. Put those together and there is a window during the
bake in which the starch has opened up and become digestible while the enzyme
is still alive and working. In that window amylase cuts starch into
dextrins --- short, sweet, water-holding fragments that never set. The loaf
bakes, browns, sounds right, and has a sticky unset core that a knife will not
get through cleanly. It is not
underbaked. It has been eaten from the inside.

Acid closes that window from both ends. Dropping the dough to around pH 4.3
suppresses alpha-amylase sharply, and it raises the temperature at which rye
starch gelatinises, so the starch becomes available later and to a much weaker
enzyme. That is the whole argument for a rye sour, and it is why the sour is
not optional the way a wheat levain is. In week 6 you chose a starter for
flavour and timing; here you are choosing it to stop the loaf destroying itself.
Commercial bakers who skip the sour acidify with lactic acid or a dose of
acidified dry sour instead --- same mechanism, bought in a bag.

The rest of the method follows from the first two paragraphs. A sour of 20 to
40% of the total flour, held at 26--28 °C for 12 to 16 hours, is what reliably
gets the whole dough acid enough. A paste is handled with wet tools and put in
a tin because there is nothing to shape. A dense, wet crumb that conducts heat
poorly needs an hour or more in the oven. And the crumb finishes setting well
after the loaf leaves it, as the starch gel retrogrades and binds the water
still loose in it, which is why the 24-hour wait before cutting is part of the
bake rather than good manners.

- rye's proteins, and why having them is not the same as being able to use them
