Last week ended with an instruction to shape decisively. This week that
instruction stops working. Take the baguette from 70% up to 78% and the dough
will not take a seam, will not hold a boule, and will not survive being handled
the way a baguette wants to be handled. Almost everything about ciabatta follows
from that one change.

## What water does

At 65%, week 1's dough, the gluten network is concentrated enough to behave
like a solid: push it and it pushes back, shape it and it stays. At 78% the same
network exists but is spread through a fifth more water, so it behaves like a
very slow liquid. It flows to fill whatever it is in, and left alone on a bench
it spreads. Gliadin's extensibility dominates and glutenin's elasticity has
less to work with, so the dough stretches a long way and snaps back weakly.

That slackness is not a defect to be corrected. It is the condition for the
crumb. Bubble walls made of dilute, extensible gluten can be blown out thin and
wide without tearing; the same gas in a 65% dough produces the small even cells
you baked in week 1. You cannot get a ciabatta crumb out of a stiff dough at
any fermentation length.

## Strength in stages

Kneading a 78% dough by hand does almost nothing useful. There is no resistance
to work against, and twenty minutes of slapping produces a tired baker and a
dough that is still soup.

Folding works instead because it is intermittent. A coil fold takes about ten
seconds and stretches the whole mass in two directions at once, aligning the
gluten strands that fermentation and hydration have been forming on their own
in the intervening half hour. A stretch-and-fold gets to the same place by
pulling: lift one edge, stretch it up and over the centre, turn the tub, repeat.
It works, but each pull drags across the surface and presses the fold flat,
where a coil lets the dough fold under its own weight. The coil costs less gas,
so the coil is the one we use. Four folds at thirty-minute intervals give you a
dough you can lift in one piece. The interval is doing as much work as the fold
— you are letting the network build itself and then organising it, rather than
forcing it.

Folds also leave the gas alone, which matters more here than in any dough so
far. Kneading a risen dough degasses it by design. Folding a risen dough traps
fresh air at the seams and leaves the existing bubbles intact, which is why
folded doughs get visibly puffier over the bulk while stiffer doughs mostly get
stronger.

The preferment sits underneath all of this. A poolish at 100% hydration is
already at the dough's own water level, so it dissolves in without fighting;
a biga at 50% is stiffer, ferments more slowly and acidifies differently, and
Italian bakeries use it for exactly that reason. Either way roughly 40% of the
flour arrives already fermented, which buys flavour and some organic acid
without adding hours to the day.

## Everything is decided at shaping

Two and a half hours of bulk fermentation produces a tub full of gas. Shaping
is the twenty seconds in which you either keep it or throw it away.

So: flour the bench heavily, tip the dough out rather than pulling it, cut
straight down with a bench knife, and move the pieces with your hands flat
underneath. Wet hands where you must touch it, dry flour where the dough must
not stick. No punching down, no pre-shape, no rounding, no seam. The rectangles
are rectangles because that is the shape the dough is already in. Dimpling is
the second and last pressure the dough gets: fingertips only, on a piece domed
high enough that it would bake unevenly.

Three failures cover most of what goes wrong:

- **The puddle.** No folds, or folds too late. The dough never developed enough
  strength to hold its own height, so it spreads to 15 mm on the couche and
  bakes flat. Water without structure is not hydration, it is soup.
- **The tight crumb.** The dough was strong enough, and then someone degassed
  it at shaping — pressed it out, folded it, rounded it. The holes were there an
  hour earlier. Handling removed them.
- **The gummy centre.** The loaf came out at 90 °C. The crust is coloured and
  the outer crumb is fine, but starch does not all gelatinise at one
  temperature. A crumb holds less water than the starch wants, so the process
  that began around 60 °C is still finishing at 90 °C, and the middle has
  neither set nor driven off the water it is carrying. Bake to 98 °C, then cool
  for forty minutes, because a correctly baked ciabatta cut hot smears anyway.

## Outline

- 65% to 78% across three weeks: extensibility up, elasticity down, dough
  flows instead of holding
- why kneading fails on a wet dough and intermittent folding does not
- coil folds against stretch-and-folds — strength in stages, gas left alone
- poolish at 100% against biga at 50%: flavour, acidity, and 40% of the flour
  pre-fermented
- wet hands, floured bench, and where each belongs
- cut and dimple: the only two pressures the dough is allowed
- the puddle, the tight crumb and the gummy centre
