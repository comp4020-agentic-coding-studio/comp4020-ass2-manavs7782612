Yeast is a population. You feed it, keep it warm, and read it like a gauge,
which is what the last six weeks have been about. Bicarbonate of soda is a white
powder that does one thing, once, the instant it gets wet, and then it is
finished. There is no culture to judge, no schedule to hold to, and no window in
which the batter improves. The whole method is arranged around a reaction you
cannot slow down.

## One reaction, and it has already started

Bicarbonate of soda is sodium bicarbonate, NaHCO₃, and it is a base. Put it in
water with an acid and it gives up carbon dioxide:

NaHCO₃ + H⁺ → Na⁺ + H₂O + CO₂

The arithmetic is worth carrying around. Sodium bicarbonate has a molar mass of
84 and carbon dioxide 44, so a gram of soda that finds enough acid yields 0.52 g
of gas — 0.012 moles, which is about 290 mL at room temperature and around
360 mL by the time the centre of the loaf is approaching 100 °C. A quarter
teaspoon in a small loaf is a great deal of carbon dioxide.

Note what is not in that equation: time, warmth, food, or anything alive. The
reaction runs at room temperature in a bowl, and a good part of it is over
inside two or three minutes. That single fact sets the shape of the whole
method. You cannot mix the batter in the morning and bake it at lunchtime,
because the gas will have left through the surface while you were away. Mix,
fill, bake — ten minutes, not an hour.

## Soda needs an acid; powder brings its own

With no acid to react with, soda does very little that you want. It will
decompose thermally above about 80 °C into sodium carbonate, water and carbon
dioxide, which does raise the batter a little, but sodium carbonate is strongly
alkaline and tastes of it. A recipe calling for soda is telling you there is
acid elsewhere in the ingredient list. Buttermilk, yoghurt and sour cream; brown
sugar, which is acidic because of its molasses; honey; citrus juice; natural
cocoa powder — but not Dutch-processed cocoa, which has been alkalised and will
leave your soda with nothing to do. And very ripe fruit.

Baking powder is the same base with the problem solved in the tin. It is sodium
bicarbonate, one or two powdered acids, and cornflour as a dry filler that keeps
them apart and takes up stray moisture so the tin does not fizz on the shelf.
Add liquid and it is self-sufficient. A single-acting powder uses monocalcium
phosphate, which is fast and cold-reacting: roughly two thirds of its gas is
gone within two minutes of wetting. A double-acting powder adds a second acid,
usually sodium acid pyrophosphate or sodium aluminium sulfate, that needs heat
before it does anything, so only a small fraction leaves at the bench and most
of the gas waits for the oven. That is the margin that forgives slow bench work,
and it is why almost every powder on a supermarket shelf is double-acting.

Because most of a teaspoon of powder is acid and starch, it carries far less
soda than a teaspoon of soda does, and the ratios differ accordingly.

| Raising agent | Starting ratio | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baking powder | 1 tsp per 120 g flour | needs only liquid |
| Bicarbonate of soda | ¼ tsp per 120 g flour | needs an acid in the batter |

Treat both as starting points rather than limits. The soda figure scales with
the acid in the batter, not only with the flour: a loaf carrying 400 g of very
ripe banana and 150 g of brown sugar has far more acid in it than that baseline
assumes, and will take half as much soda again before any is left unreacted.

Many recipes use both agents, and that is not hedging. The soda neutralises acid
that would otherwise leave the crumb tight and pale, and the powder tops up the
gas without shifting the pH any further.

## What too much tastes like

Overdosing the soda is the commonest fault in a quick bread, and it announces
itself three ways at once. The flavour first: soapy, faintly metallic, bitter on
the finish, from the sodium carbonate left over once every available acid has
been consumed. Then the colour, because raising the pH accelerates Maillard
browning — the crumb comes out darker than its bake time should give, sometimes
with a grey-green cast where the alkali has met the pigments in cocoa or fruit.

Then the shape. Too much gas arrives too early, before the starch has
gelatinised or the egg has set, so the loaf inflates fast, domes hard, splits
wide, and then sinks through the middle as it cools because nothing had
solidified to hold the volume. A crater down the centre of a banana bread is
almost always soda, not the oven.

## Gluten is now the fault

Week 7 took the gluten away from you: rye has almost none, so there was nothing
to knead and nothing to look for in a windowpane. This week is the harder case.
There is plenty of gluten-forming protein in the bowl, it will form a network
the moment you give it enough agitation, and you have to leave it alone.

The reason is timing. In a yeasted dough you want a continuous elastic network,
because it has to retain gas over hours. In a quick bread the gas arrives in
minutes and the loaf is set within the hour, and an elastic network does two
harmful things to it: it resists expansion, giving a tough, close, rubbery
crumb, and it channels the gas that does move into long vertical tunnels instead
of letting it spread as small round cells.

So the method suppresses gluten at every step. Plain flour at around 9% protein
rather than the bakers' flour you have used since week 1. Fat and sugar coating
the flour particles so the proteins hydrate less readily and any strands that do
form stay short. And above all the muffin method: whisk all the dry ingredients
in one bowl, combine all the wet in another, then pour the wet into the dry and
fold only until the last of the dry flour has disappeared. A dozen folds. The
batter should still be lumpy when you stop. Those lumps hydrate in the oven and
you will never find them; the tunnels left by beating the batter smooth will be
in the loaf for as long as it lasts.

## Why the bananas have to be black

The fruit is a leavening ingredient, not a flavouring. As a banana ripens its
own amylase converts starch to sugar — from roughly 20% starch and 2% sugar in
green fruit to the reverse once the skin has blackened. Pectin in the cell walls
degrades, so the flesh mashes to a purée instead of staying in nuggets that bake
into wet pockets. The pH falls to around 4.8, which is the acid your soda is
counting on. And the fruit is about three-quarters water, which is why this
batter needs no milk.

Ripening is enzymatic, so heat cannot rush it. An hour in a 150 °C oven will
blacken a green banana's skin and soften its flesh, and it will still be
starchy, bland and short of acid, because the enzymes were denatured long before
they had done any work.

## Sugar is the reason it keeps

A quick bread runs 50 to 70% sugar on flour weight, well past anything in the
lean doughs. Sugar sweetens, but it is also hygroscopic and it competes with the
flour for water, which limits gluten development, raises the temperature at
which the starch gelatinises, and tenderises the crumb. The consequence you will
notice is shelf life. Staling is largely starch retrogradation — amylose and
amylopectin recrystallising and pushing water out of the crumb — and sugar bound
to that water slows the process considerably. An unsweetened tin loaf is past
its best the morning after you bake it. A banana bread is at its best on day two
and perfectly good on day four.

## A moderate oven, and the crack

170 to 180 °C for 50 to 60 minutes in a 900 g tin. The restraint is geometric.
The loaf is deep, it is close to half water, and heat has to travel to the
centre before the crust is set and coloured. Bake it at 200 °C and the outside
is dark at twenty minutes with the middle still batter — pull it then and it
runs; leave it until the skewer is clean and the crust is bitter.

Test the centre with a thin skewer: moist crumbs are done, wet batter is not,
and a completely dry skewer means you have gone a few minutes too far. The
centre reads about 96 °C.

The split down the top is not a fault to design out. The crust sets after
fifteen or twenty minutes while the interior is still expanding, and the surface
tears along its weakest line. A quick bread with a flat, unbroken top usually
did not get enough lift.
