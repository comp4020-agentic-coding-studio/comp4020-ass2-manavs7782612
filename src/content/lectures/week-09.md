Week 4 put 5% olive oil into a focaccia and called it the edge of lean. Brioche
runs at 50 to 70% butter on flour weight. That is not the same idea scaled up;
past roughly 10% fat, the dough stops behaving like the ones you have been
making and needs a different method to get it made at all.

Fat is the first of the three. It films the gluten strands as they form and
lets them slide past each other instead of bonding, which is why enriched crumb
tears short and stays soft for days. The cost is development time: the same
lubrication that makes the crumb tender also makes the network slow to build, so
a brioche takes two to three times as long in the mixer as week 1's straight
dough. It is also why the order of operations is not negotiable. Build the
gluten with flour, liquid and egg until the dough windowpanes, and only then add
softened butter a piece at a time. Add it at the start and the proteins are
coated before they have linked, and the dough never comes together at all.

Sugar is the second, and it works on the yeast rather than the flour. At 10 to
12% on flour weight, dissolved sugar raises the osmotic pressure of the dough
water high enough to draw water back out of the yeast cells through their
membranes. Dehydrated yeast ferments slowly, so enriched doughs carry two to
three times the yeast of a lean dough, ferment for longer, or both. This is a
real enough problem in commercial baking that osmotolerant strains are sold
specifically for it — selected for holding their internal water against a sweet
dough, and noticeably sluggish in a plain one.

Egg is third, and it is two ingredients that arrived in the same shell. The yolk
carries fat and lecithin, an emulsifier that keeps that fat dispersed through a
water-based dough rather than separating out of it, which is most of why a
yolk-rich dough feels silky. The white is roughly 90% water and 10% protein: it
hydrates the flour and its proteins set in the oven, adding structure that
partly offsets the gluten the butter has softened. Swap whole eggs for yolks and
you get a richer, shorter, more fragile crumb.

Temperature holds the whole thing together, and the window is narrow. Butter
plasticises around 18°C and starts rendering near 26°C, so an enriched dough is
worked below about 24°C and chilled the moment it climbs. An overnight retard at
4°C is not for flavour here the way it was in week 4 — it is the only thing that
makes a 60% butter dough firm enough to divide and braid.

Challah is the useful comparison: egg and oil, no dairy, and a good deal less
fat, so it mixes fast, shapes warm and forgives more. Its braid is structure you
can see, and the technique matters — three, four or six strands, braided from
the centre out so both ends finish the same size. Then egg wash for gloss and a
moderate oven, around 180°C, because sugar and egg brown early and a hot oven
will hand you a dark loaf that is raw in the middle.

## Outline

- fat films the strands: tenderness bought with development time
- why butter goes in last, and what a dough built the other way looks like
- 50 to 70% butter on flour weight, and the 24°C ceiling
- sugar and osmotic pressure: slow yeast, more yeast, osmotolerant strains
- yolk as fat plus lecithin, white as water plus setting protein
- the overnight retard as a shaping tool, not a flavour one
- challah: leaner, dairy-free, and braided from the centre out
- egg wash, 180°C, and the dark-outside-raw-inside failure
